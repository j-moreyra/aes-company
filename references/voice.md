# Voice

The single source of truth for anything drafted as William. Read it before writing a message on
his behalf. Don't fake his voice on external content, client email, LinkedIn, website copy,
without showing him a draft first.

Reconciled 2026-08-10 from two documents that had drifted apart: this file, and
`Bill_Email_Style_Guide.md` from the `j-moreyra/email-style-guide` repo. That repo is superseded.
Where the two disagreed, William's ruling is recorded in **Where the sources disagreed** at the
bottom, along with what changed.

## The two rules that must not be wrong

**1. The name switches by audience. Nothing else changes it.**

| Audience | Greeting | Sign-off name |
|---|---|---|
| Kukla (supplier) | `Hi Patrik!` | **William** |
| Clients and prospects | `Hi Patrick,` | **Bill** |

Not by register, not by language, not by how well he knows them. A cold prospect who has never
replied still gets **Bill**. A Spanish or Portuguese thread with a client still gets **Bill**.
The only people who get **William** are Kukla.

Getting this backwards is the most visible possible error.

The greeting punctuation is a tendency, not a law. Kukla mail usually opens `Hi Patrik!` and
client mail `Hi Patrick,`, but sample 3 below is a real message to Jakob at Kukla that opens with
a comma. Follow the pattern; don't contort a draft to obey it.

**2. No em dashes.** Use a comma, a full stop, or restructure the sentence. An en dash `–` shows
up now and then and is fine, mostly in shipping terms and inline tags. See the disagreements
section for why this rule reads as counterfactual against the samples, and why it still stands.

## Core register

Plain, warm, direct. He writes like a person, not a company.

- **Short.** One to four short paragraphs, one idea each, blank line between. One-liners are
  common and welcome. The longest real sample is five sentences.
- **Direct.** The point lands in the first sentence. No ramp, no preamble, no recap of what the
  recipient already knows.
- **Warm but unfussy.** Contractions throughout: "don't", "I'll", "it's". Casual connectors:
  "Sure,", "Ok!", "Plz check numbers."
- **Action-oriented.** Ends on a clear next step ("I'll set up time on Teams next week") or a
  low-pressure invitation ("Let me know if any questions!").
- **No marketing adjectives. No hedging.** Occasionally formal verbs with Kukla
  (`kindly create a quote`).

**Structure.** State the situation, state the ask, state what happens next.

**The AI failure mode.** Drafts come out 30 to 50 percent longer than he writes, with stiffer
connectors. When in doubt, cut. If a sentence sounds like a press release, rewrite it as though he
is texting a colleague who happens to be a customer.

## Length calibration

| Context | Typical length | Example opener |
|---|---|---|
| FYI / forward | 1 line | "FYI" / "Plz check numbers." |
| Quick reply / ack | 1–2 sentences | "Thanks!" / "Sounds good!" / "Will do." |
| Status update | 2–4 sentences | "Quick update, ..." |
| Follow-up nudge | 3–5 sentences | "Hi [name], just circling back on..." |
| Technical Q&A / proposal | Bullets + 1 wrap-up line | "A few questions:" |
| Cold / intro outreach | 2–3 sentences max | "Hi [name], following up on our LinkedIn conversation..." |

## Sign-offs

The closing word does **not** switch by audience. `Cheers,` and `Best,` both appear to Kukla and
to clients. The name does, per the rule above.

| English | Spanish | Portuguese |
|---|---|---|
| Best,<br>Bill | Cordialmente,<br>Bill | Cordialmente,<br>Bill |
| Cheers,<br>Bill | Saludos,<br>Bill | Atenciosamente,<br>Bill *(rare)* |
| Thanks,<br>Bill | Saludos cordiales,<br>Bill | Abraço,<br>Bill *(warmer, with regulars)* |

To Kukla, the same closings with **William**.

Never "Sincerely", "Kind regards", "Warm regards", "Best regards". Too corporate for him.

**Habitual closers before the sign-off.** `Let me know!` / `Let me know if any questions!` Almost
every message ends on one of these.

## Follow-ups and nudges

The most-used section for recurring follow-up work. His nudges have a recognizable shape.

### English openers
- "I'm reaching out to..." *(slightly more formal variant)*
- "Quickly wanted to see if..."
- "Wanted to quickly pop in..." *(low-stakes nudges)*
- "Hey [Name], wanted to see if you've had a chance to..."

### English closers
- **"No pressure either way"** is his go-to face-saver. Use it.
- "If priorities have shifted, totally understand."
- "Just let me know either way."
- "Quick yes or no is fine, did I completely miss the mark?" *(bolder, used selectively)*

### Spanish
- "Quería saber si le gustaría retomar este tema sobre..."
- "Tienes alguna pregunta o comentario? Quedo atento."
- **"Sin presión"** is the face-saver.

### Portuguese
- "Só passando para retomar nossa conversa sobre..."
- "Queria saber se ainda há interesse..."
- **"Sem pressão de forma alguma."** is the face-saver.
- "Fico à disposição." Common closer, equivalent to "happy to help".

## Punctuation and formatting

- **No em dashes.** See the top rule.
- **Sentence-case subject lines.** Not Title Case: "New inquiry - Holcim | El Salvador"
- **Pipe character** in subject lines as a separator: "Project | Company" or "Topic | Region"
- **Job numbers inline.** `(FN: 11857)` or `F.N. 11857`, carried in the subject or first
  paragraph so the thread stays traceable.
- **Logistics spelled out in full.** `DAP – 151 Wahlstrom Rd., Savannah, GA`. Never "the usual
  address."
- **Bulleted lists** when there are 3+ items, parameters, or questions. Each bullet short, usually
  a fragment rather than a full sentence.
- **Question without a question mark** sometimes, when chatty: "Hope all's well at Victorville."
  Always a `?` for an actual question.
- **`@Name –`** inline tagging when looping someone in mid-message: "@Nico – if you can please
  share the location of center of gravity."
- **"Plz"** in internal forwards only. Externally, full "please".
- **Exclamation points** are used, never more than one per external email.
- **No emoji** in business email.
- **Calibri 11pt**, HTML body.

## What to avoid, the AI tells

Red flags that a draft sounds generated rather than written:

- **"I hope this email finds you well"**. Never used. Skip the preamble.
- **"Please don't hesitate to..."**. He says "Let me know if any questions!"
- **"I look forward to hearing from you"**. Never used. End on the actual ask.
- **"Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "Additionally"**. Use "Also," or start a new sentence.
- **"At your earliest convenience"**. Use "when you have a moment" or "whenever you can".
- **"As per our discussion"**. He writes "Per our chat" or "Following up on..."
- **"Warm regards" / "Kindest regards"**. Too formal.
- **Three-clause sentences** strung together with semicolons. He writes shorter.
- **Over-explaining context the recipient already has.** He assumes they remember the thread and
  doesn't recap unless reactivating after a long gap.

## Micro-conventions

- "Best," on its own line, name on the next. Not "Best regards,".
- Lower-case "a few questions:" before a bulleted list, not "A Few Questions:".
- "Re:" kept from the original thread when replying. Don't restart a subject line unless the topic
  genuinely shifts.
- "FYI" as a one-word body when forwarding without commentary.

## When he pushes back

Direct, bordering on blunt, always paired with warmth. Real examples:

- "Quick yes or no is fine, did I completely miss the mark?" *(to a contact gone silent)*
- "Didn't get a response, so we can reschedule for another day!" *(canceling a no-show, note the
  cheerful exclamation)*
- "Still have to add in the lift-and-dump system to the quote. Have to think about how to do it."
  *(internal, frank about open problems, no varnish)*
- "Plz check numbers." *(internal forward, terse, trusts the recipient)*

The pattern: name the issue plainly, often with humor or self-deprecation, then ask for a clear
yes or no, or just state the next move. No six sentences of softening.

## Multilingual calibration

Fully fluent in English, Spanish and Portuguese. The Spanish and Portuguese are native-speaker
quality and slightly more formal than his English, matching business norms in those languages,
but carry the same warmth and directness.

- **Spanish.** `usted` by default in business ("podría", "le gustaría", "quería saber"). `tú` only
  with people he is clearly familiar with. Latin American, not Iberian.
- **Portuguese.** Brazilian. `você` form. Common terms: "balança" (scale), "clínquer" (clinker),
  "alimentador" (feeder), "transportador" (conveyor). Correct spelling and accents: não, à, é, ã.
- **Don't translate idioms literally.** "No pressure either way" becomes "Sin presión" or
  "Sem pressão de forma alguma". Both are naturalized in his usage, not calques.
- **The name does not change with the language.** A Spanish or Portuguese client thread signs
  **Bill**. This reverses what the old style guide said; see the disagreements section.

## Writing to Kukla, vocabulary and relay rules

Added 2026-08-08 after William rewrote a drafted reply to Armin Avdibegovic. These sit on top of
everything above. They do not change the closings, which do not vary by audience.

- **Use Kukla's word, not the client's.** They say **pre-bin**. Not "refill hopper", not a literal
  rendering of the Spanish "tolva de recarga". Translating the client's term produces vocabulary
  Kukla does not use.
- **Name the equipment, not the vendor.** "The feeder", "the unit". Not "the Kukla equipment",
  they know whose equipment it is.
- **Hedge what you are relaying.** "They said it *may be* difficult", not "it *is* difficult". The
  same explicit-attribution habit below, applied to certainty as well as source.
- **Open on the substance.** He cut "Thanks for quotation 260824" from a draft. The subject line
  already carries the number.
- **Ask them to "advise whether"**, not "tell me whether", when the question is technical.

**Attribution is explicit, in both directions.** When relaying he names the source: "The client
would like to know…", "I mentioned this to the client, and yes, they are aware…". Never blurs who
asked for what. That is the relay job showing.

Why it matters: Kukla is a 90-year manufacturer and the relationship is peer-to-peer engineering,
not vendor management. Borrowed client vocabulary and soft filler read as imprecise to them.

## Pre-send checklist

- [ ] Name correct for the audience? **Bill** to a client or prospect, **William** to Kukla.
- [ ] Any em dashes? Remove them.
- [ ] Under four sentences, or replaceable with bullets? If not, cut.
- [ ] `Hi [Name],` or Hola / Olá / Buenas / Bom dia? Never "Dear [Name]".
- [ ] Does the first sentence get to the point, rather than stalling on "I hope..." / "I wanted to..."?
- [ ] Face-saver included if this is a follow-up? "No pressure either way" / "Sin presión" /
      "Sem pressão"
- [ ] Sign-off one of "Best," / "Cheers," / "Cordialmente," / "Saludos,"? Never "Sincerely" or
      "Warm regards".
- [ ] Job number carried, if the thread has one?
- [ ] Calibri 11pt?
- [ ] All original recipients kept, To and CC, when replying to a thread?

## Where the sources disagreed

Recorded so nobody re-litigates it. William ruled on both, 2026-08-10.

**1. What decides the name.** This file said it switches by **audience**: William to Kukla, Bill
to clients. The style guide said it switches by **register**: Bill for contacts already known,
William for cold outreach, new contacts, and all Spanish and Portuguese threads.

They collide exactly where the follow-up automation works, on prospects who have not replied.
**Audience wins.** Clients get Bill, cold or not, in any language. Two consequences worth naming:

- Spanish and Portuguese client mail now signs **Bill**. The style guide defaulted those to
  William in two separate places.
- The old checklist item "Name match the language register?" is gone. Language is no longer an
  input to the name.

**2. Em dashes.** This file said none. The style guide said "em dashes everywhere," used liberally
as connectors, and its checklist asked whether one had been used at least once.

**No em dashes wins.** Note honestly what that means: the guide's claim was descriptive, drawn
from real sent mail, and the samples above do contain em dashes. The rule is prescriptive. He is
correcting a habit, not describing one. Don't "restore" em dashes on the grounds that the samples
have them, and don't edit the samples to remove them.

**3. Scope, which is why one file is better than two.** The style guide never mentioned Kukla
once, in 1,955 words. This file was Kukla-heavy and thin on client outreach, with nothing in
Spanish or Portuguese. Neither covered the job alone. The merged document does.

**Still outstanding.** The Weekly HubSpot Follow-ups routine
(`trig_01K9Bsr6PfekZRNSaJmi1QRV`) still clones `j-moreyra/email-style-guide` and reads the
superseded file, and its own prompt restates both losing rules inline. Until that routine is
repointed, it drafts against rules this document overrules.
